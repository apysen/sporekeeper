# Changelog: 2026-08-23

## Walls dim growth instead of deleting everything behind them

- Seed and Bloom spreading tested the straight line from the stamp's source to
  every cell and refused the cell outright if anything blocked it. One clipped
  corner therefore erased everything behind it, and the wedge widened with
  distance: a ridge on a basalt rock cost far more spread than the ridge was worth.
- Obstruction now attenuates rather than blocks. The same ray walk counts the
  blocked cells it crosses and scales the stamp by `obstruction_falloff_per_cell`
  (0.45) for each, stopping at zero after three. A corner clipped once delivers
  55% of its material, twice delivers 10%, and solid rock still takes everything.
  Measured on one stamp beside a rock: of 21 cells the old rule refused, 8 now
  receive growth and the rest are behind two or more cells of stone.
- It costs nothing. The stress probe reads 6.67ms average and a 15.4ms peak,
  against 6.79ms and 15.6ms for the binary rule it replaces.

## What I tried first, and why it is not in the build

- The first attempt measured true geodesic distance: a Dijkstra out from each
  stamp through claimable ground, so material genuinely flowed around obstacles.
  It worked and it looked right, and it cost six times the frame budget — the
  stress probe went from a 15.6ms peak to 94.6ms.
- Rewriting it as a chamfer distance transform over a reusable window brought that
  to 30ms, inlining the obstacle checks did not move it, and gating it by stamp
  size and by proximity to obstacles did not either. An A/B with everything else
  held constant confirmed the transform itself was the cost: 16.4ms peak with it
  reverted, 29.6ms with it in.
- The attenuated ray reaches the same visual result — growth wrapping a rock
  rather than ending in a hard wedge — for the price of the walk that was already
  being done. Kept the cheap one.

## The Bloom splatter finally dents hostile ground

Reported three times, and I had twice fixed something that was not the cause. The
measurements that settled it:

- Real globs land carrying **0.00–0.18 Mass**, not the 30 my isolated test used.
  The maturity-break coefficient had been calibrated against a made-up number, so
  in play it delivered a break of 0.005–0.22 where roughly 2.0 was needed to show.
  Recalibrated against measured values.
- That was not the limiter either. Raising the landing Mass budget from 6× to 40×
  moved the dent by **two cells**, which ruled Mass out entirely.
- The limiter was footprint. A glob stamped its landing at the radius it had been
  flying at — `radius_cells` of **0.8 to 3.6** — so nine droplet-sized footprints
  could not add up to a hole regardless of what they carried. The whole splatter
  touched 55 cells.

Paint does not land at the size it flew at: a droplet flattens across the surface
into a splat several times its own diameter, and further the faster it arrives,
which is the Weber number of the impact — the droplet's momentum against the
surface tension holding it together. Landings now spread by `landing_spread_scale`
(3.2× the flight radius) plus a term in the root of impact speed.

- A Bloom thrown at a fully mature red front now dents **443 cells**, up from 55.
- Against a control: red gains 890 cells in four seconds unaided, and only 169
  when a Bloom lands in it.
- The stress probe is unchanged at 6.686ms average, 15.557ms peak.

## The glob is the paint

Two behaviours contradicted that and are gone:

- Globs no longer dissolve in flight. `hostile_mass_drain_per_pixel` spent a
  glob's material for every pixel of hostile ground it crossed, so a throw into a
  thick invasion arrived with nothing — the opposite of paint, which crosses enemy
  ground and lands on it. Hostile ground still slows a glob hard; it now costs the
  throw distance rather than material.
- Landings carry a **maturity ceiling of zero**. Thrown paint is wet material
  sitting on top of the ground rather than territory that grew there, so the Green
  it wins has no root behind it. In the four-second measurement above the front
  takes most of it back on its own, which is the intended counterweight to a splat
  that can now carve a real hole: the throw opens ground, and the run has to
  follow it up to keep it.

Regression test added at the level the bug actually lived at — a real Bloom
against a real mature front, asserting a dent of 250+ cells. The old unit test of
the same mechanism passed throughout while the feature did not work.

## Explosion pitch

Each plant explosion now detunes itself between 0.90× and 1.12×, drawn from a
random source kept separate from the gameplay RNG so audio can never shift a run.
The band is deliberately under a 1.5 ratio so twelve blasts in a row still read as
one sound rather than a scale.

## Bloom becomes throw-led, at the strength it had before any of this

The splatter was the payload by accident and then by overcorrection. Two structural
changes, and then a calibration back to where the path started.

**The material is now divided rather than duplicated.** `mass` was being passed in
full to the central surge *and* in full to the droplets, so every Bloom created
twice the material it was given. It is now split: `central_material_share` (0.28)
stays as a puddle at the detonation point — the part of the charge that never got
airborne, the blast's footprint rather than its payload — and the rest is thrown.
The central radius drops to 0.34 of the nominal surge.

**Droplet size and count are no longer independent.** The first attempt scaled both
with the run's Mass line, which double-counts the material and produced a curve
that ran away with the arena: at a fully invested Mass line one Bloom took 3378
cells. They are not free of each other. Size comes from the specific energy of the
blast — pile more material behind the same detonation and there is less energy per
unit of what it is tearing apart, so the breakup coarsens — and the count then
follows from conservation, because the material has to be exactly used up by the
droplets it became. Coverage therefore grows as the Mass ratio to the power of one
minus twice the size exponent, which is what makes the line an investment rather
than a runaway:

| Mass line | globs | green | dent |
|---|---|---|---|
| ×1.1 | 6 | +139 | 125 |
| ×3.0 | 8 | +336 | 279 |
| ×10.2 | 11 | +768 | 501 |

**Calibrated to the pre-splatter Bloom.** Measured the original surge-led Bloom as
the reference — all material in the central surge, full radius, nothing thrown —
and matched an uninvested throw-led Bloom to it:

| | open ground | mature front |
|---|---|---|
| pre-splatter surge | +66 green, 68 dent | +38 green, 45 dent |
| throw-led, base | +55 green, 70 dent | +54 green, 68 dent |

Total path strength is the same; the profile moved. A base Bloom is slightly weaker
on open ground and substantially better against a matured front, which is the point
of the rework rather than a balance gain: the throw is the only part of a Bloom that
arrives with momentum, and momentum is what does mechanical work on established
ground instead of negotiating with its resistance.

Stress probe: 6.704ms average, 15.770ms peak, against 16.67ms.

## Inoculation — Bloom × Spore

A glob landing on a Seed that is already breaking ground establishes it. What a
Bloom throws is the ecosystem itself, propagules and all, and how much living
material reaches a site is what decides whether it takes — so a direct hit settles
it. Gated on Super Bloom, which is the pairing that already makes a germination arm
a chained detonation, so the throw carries itself into the next Bloom without a
second rule saying so. Budgeted at `inoculation_seeds_per_bloom` (2) per Bloom.

The first version cascaded: one Bloom opened all twelve Seeds on the field. A
cracked Seed reported the chain depth it had been *planted* at, so every generation
looked like the first and nothing terminated. Seeds now inherit the depth of the
Bloom that cracked them, and globs carry the depth of the Bloom that threw them.
Openings fell from 14 to 4 — the first Bloom's budget of two, one chained
generation's two more, and the third refused by `maximum_chain_depth`.

Both are covered: that the pairing gates it at all, and that the chain it starts
ends.

## Fixed: a Bloom was stripping maturity from the run's own Green

Reported, and it was mine. The maturity ceiling that keeps newly taken ground
rootless was being applied to *every* player-held cell a splat touched, not only to
ground the throw had just claimed — so firing a Bloom over your own mature
territory reset it. The comment I wrote at the time rationalised it ("paint is all
this cell has"), which is the wrong model: material settling onto established growth
becomes part of it.

Ground that changes hands still starts at zero maturity — that is the branch that
was always meant to carry the rule. Ground the run already holds is now
*reinforced* instead, and how well it roots rides the run's Maturation rather than
being a flat number, because Maturation is the ecosystem's own capacity to put down
roots and this is that capacity acting on material the Bloom delivered:

| Maturation | reinforcement | splat-centre maturity, from 0.50 |
|---|---|---|
| ×1.08 | 0.081 | 0.58 |
| ×2.00 | 0.150 | 0.65 |
| ×4.32 | 0.324 | 0.79 |

That keeps the two economy lines distinct: Mass decides how much ground a Bloom
reaches, Maturation decides how well what it lands on holds.

Three checks added — that no owned cell ever loses maturity to a Bloom, that
landing on established Green raises it against an un-reinforced control, and that
the Maturation line scales it.

## Paint runs down walls and rocks instead of blinking out

Basalt and the arena wall were sinks: a glob touching either one stopped dead and
deposited on the spot, so what the player saw was the droplet vanishing against the
rock. Both go through the same check — unclaimable ground — so one change covers
walls, basalt, and the arena edge alike.

A hit now splits along the surface, which is what actually happens. The component
driving *into* the face has nowhere to go and is what splatters there; the component
running *along* the face is untouched and carries the glob on down the surface. So a
glob thrown square at a rock still marks it hard, and one that catches it at an angle
mostly keeps going — the difference between throwing paint at a wall and dragging a
loaded brush across it.

The field gained `get_obstacle_normal_at_local`, taken as the gradient of the blocked
mask over a small window, so a flat face gives a square-on normal, an inside corner
gives the diagonal, and a lone clipped cell barely deflects anything.

**What was missing was gravity.** Deflecting the glob correctly still sent it
skating off into open ground — it ran 204px along the face but landed 6.3 cells away
from it. In life what holds paint on a wall while the rest of its motion carries it
down is gravity, and this arena is seen from above. The honest stand-in is wetting: a
liquid clings to a surface it has touched. Globs now stay wetted to the face for
`wall_cling_seconds` and are held against it, which puts them 2.0 cells from the rock
when they finally pool instead of 6.3.

Measured on a Bloom detonated against basalt: **+76 green against the old +77**, with
three wall contacts. The behaviour changed; the output did not.

### Two things this cost, and what they cost

- **Charging the trail per splat rather than per pixel.** A streak lays its marks
  closer together, and the trail spent a share of *remaining* Mass at each one, so
  tripling the marks made the glob bleed out exponentially and arrive carrying
  nothing — a wall hit went to −2 green. The trail is now charged by distance
  covered, so what a glob spends per pixel is the same whether it is streaking a
  face or crossing open ground.
- **A full-sized splat at every contact.** Each contact queued a landing-sized stamp
  at 3.2× spread, up to four per glob, and large stamps are expensive because each
  cell walks an obstruction ray. The stress probe started spiking to 19–20ms against
  a 16.67ms budget. The splash footprint now scales with how square-on the hit was,
  and a contact under `minimum_face_splash_share` leaves only its streak — a glancing
  graze should not stamp a full mark anyway. Peaks are back to 15.5–16.6ms, matching
  the baseline with the feature switched off.

Two checks added: that paint thrown at an obstacle travels along the face instead of
stopping dead, and that it stays against the face rather than glancing away.

## The ecosystem axes now show what they do

Buying a level called `territory_field.overgrow` once, which spent the whole budget
in a single simulation step. The border jumped a visible distance between two
frames — the number changing rather than the ecosystem growing. And the `overgrown`
signal had nothing connected to it, so Pressure and Cohesion had no visual at all;
Mass only read because it moved `_control` hard enough to see.

**The pour.** `overgrow` is split into `sample_overgrow_frontier` and
`apply_overgrow`, so a release scans the field once, keeps the border it was aimed
at, and is delivered over `injection_ramp_seconds` on a smoothstep. The total is
unchanged — measured against the old one-shot on identical state, **1.3200 both**.

**Three axes, three actions**, drawn along the border they were poured into rather
than as three tints of one flash:

- **Mass** — a bead that swells at the frontier and settles. Material arriving.
- **Pressure** — a chevron that leaves the border and travels outward, so the axis
  reads as motion. This one had been invisible entirely.
- **Cohesion** — stitches laid *along* the border, closing as they brighten.
  Tangential on purpose: nothing else in the game draws along the frontier, so the
  axis owns that read.

**What the ramp cost, and what fixed it.** Delivering a slice every frame touches
every sampled cell 60 times a second, and releases overlap. The stress peak went
from 15.6ms to 16.2–16.8ms against a 16.67ms budget — isolated by setting the ramp
to zero and back. It is now delivered in `injection_slice_count` steps (12) across
the ramp instead of once per frame: the border is redrawn every frame regardless and
the simulation diffuses between steps, so it looks the same. Peak is back to
15.4–16.0ms.

## Progress bars

HP, Green Territory, Level, and the three ecosystem meters now use the wenrexa bar
art. The ecosystem meters were ASCII (`█░`) inside the button text and are now real
`ProgressBar` nodes.

Two mistakes on the way in, both mine:

- **The gradient was destroyed.** I set `texture_margin_top/bottom` to 10, which
  splits the texture into a 3×3 grid and stretches the middle band vertically. The
  art's gradient runs top to bottom, so stretching a thin slice of it flattened the
  bar into cap-colour, flat middle, cap-colour. Vertical margins are now zero, so
  the full-height gradient maps straight onto the bar.
- **The shape read as a pill.** At 20px against 37px native art, the 10px end caps
  were most of the bar. Bars are now 32px, near native, and read as the rounded
  rectangle the art actually is.

UI textures are scaled to fit their controls, and linear filtering softened every
edge; the top-level Controls under `Interface` now set `texture_filter = 1`
(nearest), which everything beneath inherits.

## The editor and the game now agree

Standing rule from the user: what they edit in the scene is what the game should
show. A placeholder describing an older version of its own line makes the editor
lie about the layout being arranged in it.

Audited all 42 declared `text` values against what a fresh run writes into the same
node. Five disagreed, three of them mine from today:

- the three ecosystem buttons still carried the ASCII meter (`●○○○○○○○   0 / 10`)
  that a real `ProgressBar` replaced, and the organ names rather than the axis names
  the drawer speaks in;
- `RunLabel` described a two-line shape the wave header no longer writes;
- `MetabolismLabel` and `RelicsLabel` were both a rewrite behind.

A check now holds this: every declared placeholder must match the shape the game
writes into it, compared with digits normalised so a live number moving is not a
disagreement. The draft panel is exempt and says why — it is filled from whatever
the run rolls, so its placeholders can only ever be an example.

The tray's Biomass line is the same rule applied in the other direction: rather than
teach the scene about the runtime `BIOMASS` heading, the heading is gone and the line
is the single line the scene already declared.

## The ecosystem buttons place their own contents

A `Button` centres whatever is in its `text` property and offers no way to move it,
so there was nothing to adjust. Each axis in the drawer is now a
`PhaseBMetabolismButton`: the wording is drawn by a child `Caption` label that the
button positions, and the button keeps only the press behaviour and the background.

Runs as a tool script, following the upgrade row, so the offsets apply in the editor
as well as in the run — the drawer can be arranged where it is being looked at,
which is the point.

Knobs, all in whole pixels because a Control's rect is snapped to the pixel grid and
a fractional offset moves some rows and not others:

- **Text Placement** — `text_offset_x`, `text_offset_y`, `text_alignment`.
- **Meter Placement** — `meter_offset_x`, `meter_offset_y`, `meter_inset`,
  `meter_height`, `meter_bottom_margin`. The bar art is 37 pixels tall, so
  `meter_height` decides how near its own proportions it is drawn.

The meter no longer needs a blank line reserved for it in the wording, since it is
placed rather than flowed, and `display_text` carries a sample so the editor shows a
filled button.

### The offsets were the wrong idea and are gone

Placing the meter in the editor did nothing: the script recomputed its rect from the
exported offsets in `_apply_layout` on every load, so anything dragged by hand was
silently undone. Confirmed rather than guessed — the scene had the meter at
`offset_bottom = -12` while the button still declared the default
`meter_bottom_margin` of 4, and the script was snapping it back on load.

A tool script that owns a child's rect cannot coexist with arranging that child in
the editor, and the editor is where this is being arranged. All the placement
exports are removed. The caption and the meter are placed in the scene, by hand, and
the script's only job now is to hold the wording, mirror it into the `Caption`, and
keep the Button's own `text` empty so nothing draws underneath in a place nothing can
move it from.

A check holds the general rule: **nothing moves a control the scene placed by hand.**
It compares every declared `offset_*` against where the control ends up in a live run,
which is the layout half of the same rule the placeholder check covers for wording.


## Note: the editor overwrites scene edits made from outside it

While the scene is open in Godot, a save from the editor rewrites the whole file and
drops changes made to it on disk. All seven `texture_filter = 1` lines — the nearest
filtering that fixed the blurry button and bar edges — were stripped this way and had
to be re-applied. Worth knowing in both directions: edits to a `.tscn` from outside
the editor are not safe while it is open, and neither are the editor's while a script
is writing to the same file.

## Fixed: the injection ramp had no ceiling

Every purchase queues a release, and nothing bounded the queue — a run that buys
without the clock advancing between piles them up, and each frame pays for every one
still open, each touching up to 640 cells. Four metabolism checks failed and the
smoke suite went from **33 seconds to over ten minutes**. The queue caps at six now;
past that the oldest is delivered in full at once, which is what a burst of purchases
should look like anyway. Suite back to 33 seconds, all checks passing.

## Fixed: nearest filtering was applied to text

Setting `texture_filter = 1` on the panels fixed the blurry bar and button edges and
quietly wrecked every label inside them — a CanvasItem's filter is inherited by
everything under it, and glyphs are textures like anything else, so the panels were
point-sampling their own text. It now sits on the six progress bars alone, which
carry no text.

Worth separating from that: the run label's own softness is not filtering. It is a
`scale` of 2.475 on a `font_size` of 10 — glyphs rasterised at ten pixels and then
magnified. Raising the font size and returning the scale to 1 is the fix, and that is
the user's to make in the scene.

## Phase 0 of the Root Metabolism rework: the purchase system goes behind a flag

- `docs/root_and_hud_implementation_guide.md` is now the master spec for the
  incremental layer. Root stops being a selectable Path and becomes the
  infrastructure every build grows on: held Green roots, rooted Green produces
  Biomass into per-component reserves, and Biomass is spent either on the
  battlefield now or on permanent physiology.
- Its Phase 0 asks for the old economy to be switched off behind a flag rather
  than deleted, so the two can be compared. `PhaseBMetabolism.purchase_system_enabled`
  does that. Off, no axis unlocks however long the run goes, nothing is buyable at
  any Reserve, and no Breakthrough is offered.
- The flag reaches everything through `is_system_unlocked`, which is the one
  question every caller already asks. The drawer empties itself with no HUD code
  needing to know the flag exists. `get_unlocked_system_count` was reading the raw
  `_unlocked` array and so reported Mass as available while the rest of the
  component refused to sell it; it goes through the same chokepoint now.
- Nothing is bought while it is off, so every multiplier the field reads is already
  neutral at level 1 and the simulation is untouched. Overgrow deliberately
  survives the flag: the doc keeps it and converts it into the first metabolic
  discharge, so it has to stay playable.
- Root Path selection needed no new work. `progression.offer_root_path` was already
  the switch Phase 0 asks for.
- The suite pins the flag on beside the existing path switches, so a narrowed
  playtest cannot quietly change what the checks cover. Two new checks: the flag
  severs the purchase surface without disturbing Overgrow or the field, and
  flagging it back on restores the economy exactly. 252 passing, no failures.


## Phase 1: root depth is the maturity the field already simulates

- The doc's Phase 1 asks for a `root_depth` field and a debug visualization, and
  answers to one question: does sustained territory visibly become established?
- No new per-cell array was added, because the field already runs the doc's own
  pseudocode. Maturity grows while a cell keeps its owner, decays while the cell is
  neutral, and resets when the owner flips. At the authored tuning that is roughly
  24s of holding to become Mature and about 3s of contest to lose it, which is
  already the distinction between touching ground and keeping it. A parallel
  `root_depth` would have been a second copy of that to keep in step, on a field
  where §17 says to stay cheap.
- Named the four stages the doc asks a player to read off the ground — Shallow,
  Established, Developed, Late — as bands of maturity rather than new state. Their
  lower boundary is `mature_threshold * 0.5`, the same split Pruning already
  harvests in, so the stages and the harvest order cannot drift apart. Only the top
  boundary needed authoring: `deep_root_threshold`, 0.88.
- New `ROOT DEPTH` debug view, banded rather than ramped. The existing MATURITY view
  is a smooth gradient, which cannot answer whether a patch has crossed into being
  established; discrete bands can. Hostile ground is banded on the same rule in its
  own hue, so a contested front does not read as if only one side ages. Reachable on
  F5 in the Phase B prototype, where the loop actually lives, not only in the
  territory sandbox where the debug fields already were.
- Instrumentation from §43: `rooted_territory_percent` and `deep_rooted_percent`.
  Both are shares of the whole claimable arena rather than of what the run holds.
  `mature_player_percent` uses the held-ground denominator and moves when either
  quantity or quality changes, which is the exact confusion the doc separates.
- `tests/root_stage_probe.gd` holds ground for 900 steps with no ability, no
  purchase and no injection, then renders the banded view and prints the stage
  histogram beside it. It cycles the view through the prototype rather than the
  field, so the on-screen view label agrees with the picture; driving the field
  alone left the header naming a view it was not showing.
- The probe reports the interior deep and the edges young: 620 Late, 153 Developed,
  118 Established, 4.0% rooted and 2.9% deeply rooted. Verified on a real GL device
  rather than the headless renderer, which also confirms the shader compiles.
- 288 passing across both suites, no failures.


## Phase 2: Biomass is produced by ground, and stored by the ground that produced it

- Measured before building. A full-cell pass over the authored arena's 6,026
  claimable cells costs about 1.7ms, which is the entire cost of the metrics pass, so
  a separate production pass would have doubled it. Production instead accumulates
  inside the metrics loop that was already running.
- The field now sums `control x maturity` per connected component. That is the doc's
  cell output with the productivity constant left out, because what a unit of rooted
  ground is worth is an economic decision and belongs to the economy rather than to
  the field.
- `PhaseBRootNetwork` turns that weight into a rate and pours it into the reserve of
  the component that earned it. Storage is per component, never per cell, and finite:
  a full component stops producing rather than discarding the overflow, which is what
  gives Storage Tissue something to buy later.
- Reserves survive relabelling. Topology labels are handed out in scan order and
  shuffle whenever the ecosystem changes shape, so a reserve keyed on a raw label
  would jump between unrelated regions twice a second. Each previous reserve is
  divided among the components that took its ground in proportion to how much they
  took, which divides on a split and sums on a merge with neither case special-cased.
- Naming only each component's likeliest ancestor was tried first and is wrong: a
  merge then drops the reserves of every component but the largest it swallowed, and
  the doc requires those to synchronise. Proportional overlap is what replaced it.
- The carry is gated on a topology generation counter. Applied per frame it would
  re-run a mapping whose inputs it had already consumed, redistributing reserves that
  were already indexed by the labels being mapped onto.
- The doc's central claim now measures: the same ground earns 1.63/s fresh and
  7.83/s once rooted. Territory quality is an axis separate from territory quantity.
- Behind `root_economy_enabled`, off by default, as with Phase 0. On, Progression
  mirrors the main organism's reserve so everything already reading `biomass` keeps
  working; off, the old percentage formula still drives it.
- Cost of all of it: metrics 1.69ms to 2.01ms at 5Hz, topology 0.83ms to 1.07ms at
  2Hz, about 2.1ms per simulated second. The whole-step average did not move.
- 294 passing across both suites, no failures.


## Phase 3: severed ground keeps its Biomass, and says so where it happened

- Phase 3 asks whether territory geometry matters economically. It does when a run
  cannot spend what its severed ground is holding: cutting the organism in half
  strands 2,222 of 4,339 Biomass behind the cut, and draining the main organism to
  zero does not reach any of it.
- `colony_isolated` and `colony_reconnected` are read straight off the component
  overlap the reserve carry already computes. Ground that answered to the main
  organism last pass and answers to something else now was severed; a component that
  now answers to the main organism has rejoined. Nothing has to be tracked between
  passes beyond which component was primary.
- A false reconnection had to be designed out. Which component counts as the main
  organism moves on its own - the player walks onto different green, or the
  largest-component fallback follows the biggest thing on the board - and that alone
  reported the run's own untouched territory as having just rejoined the organism.
  Reconnection now requires the current primary to be continuous with the previous
  one, so a designation moving is not mistaken for ground moving.
- Colonies are marked on the battlefield at their own position before any of it
  reaches a sidebar, as a ring with gaps torn in it rather than a tinted circle: the
  doc rules out carrying a state this important on colour alone, and a broken ring
  says "cut off" at a glance. The marker persists while the colony does, because a
  one-off flash says a colony was lost rather than that one is still out there.
- Announcements have a floor. A colony must hold 12 rooted cells or 25 Biomass to be
  worth reporting, so a torn scrap of frontier stays quiet.
- The network assumes one relabel per advance, which the run guarantees: the field
  runs a topology pass twice a second and the network advances every frame. The
  overlap only describes the most recent relabel, so consuming two at once would map
  reserves through labels that had already been replaced. The Phase 3 test forced an
  extra `analyze_topology_now` between advances and produced exactly that, which is a
  situation the running game cannot create; it now steps in the run's own order.
- Cutting deeply rooted ground is expensive, which is the system working. With every
  Root line at maximum, Mature Strength and regeneration make a band strong enough to
  divide the organism erase it instead, so the test returns to authored tuning first.
  A foothold of 165 cells cannot be severed at all - it is destroyed rather than
  divided - so the test grows an organism worth cutting before it cuts.
- `tests/colony_marker_probe.gd` renders the whole thing on a real device, since the
  headless renderer cannot confirm anything was drawn.
- 300 passing across both suites, no failures.


## The wave counter and the run clock are two labels now

- They shared one Label, and the gap between them was a run of twenty spaces inside a
  format string. Neither could be moved, recoloured, or resized without editing code
  and counting spaces.
- `WaveLabel` and `ClockLabel` are separate nodes on the side panel, each placed and

- The visual probe was writing the old label twice in a row; the first of the two was

## Phase 4: Overgrow spends the organism, and is aimed where the player is standing

- Overgrow spent a number the run carried. It now spends the main organism's reserve,
  which is a different quantity: Biomass held by a colony that is cut off is real and
  still producing, and cannot be pressed. A drained organism cannot Overgrow while its
  severed colonies are still holding thousands of Biomass, which is the tension the
  system exists to create.
- A release is aimed at the player. The front the player ran to is served first and
  whatever is left over spreads around the rest of the organism. Measured against the
  same border sampled without a focus, an aimed release lands 162 units from the
  player where an even spread lands 283.
- The aim is distance to the player, squared, against a radius. The doc asks for a
  cheap spatial approximation rather than routing, and what matters is that support
  arrives where the player went, not that it took a plausible path getting there.
  Movement is already the player's continuous input, so the metabolic layer reads it
  instead of asking for a second one.
- A release travels only through the component that paid for it, so it cannot reach a
  colony's border. That is what makes being cut off cost something rather than merely
  read differently.
- Sampling the near border and then topping up from the rest was doing a linear scan
  of what it had already taken, once per candidate. It uses a set.
- 305 passing across both suites, no failures.


## Overgrow is aimed with the mouse

- Pressing the button no longer releases anything. It asks where: the drawer gets out
  of the way, a crosshair appears, and the release goes where it is clicked. Right
  click or Escape backs out and costs nothing; Enter commits at the player, so the aim
  is never a mouse-only ability.
- The run keeps going while the player is choosing. Where to spend a reserve under
  pressure is the decision the whole layer exists to create, and pausing to plan it
  would give it away.
- The reticle leaves a gap at its centre rather than crossing lines through it, so it
  never hides the ground being aimed at. The ring around it is the reach the release
  will actually favour, and it exists only while aiming: the doc rules out a permanent
  influence circle sitting on the battlefield.
- The crosshair is clamped to the arena rather than refused outside it, so dragging
  past the edge leaves it on the nearest ground instead of stranding it where it was.
- Two things that had to be handled rather than assumed: the button keeps keyboard
  focus after being pressed, and a focused Button eats Enter before unhandled input
  sees it, which re-pressed the button instead of committing; and an aim cannot be
  left half-made across a pause or a run reset.
- The reserve can drain between opening an aim and committing it, because the run does
  not stop. Committing re-checks rather than assuming what the press saw.
- 310 passing across both suites, no failures.


## Phase 5: the player is the demand signal

- Standing on the organism pulls part of what it produces into the ground underfoot,
  where it establishes roots faster. Movement is already the player's continuous
  input, so the metabolic layer reads it rather than asking for a second one, and the
  network keeps doing the logistics.
- Ground the player stands on establishes roughly twice as fast as ground they merely
  own: 0.223 maturity gained against 0.112 at a point outside the reach, in the same
  organism over the same window.
- The share is taken out of production before it is banked, not out of the reserve
  afterwards. Taken afterwards, a component sitting at capacity discarded its
  production and then paid for support again out of what it had saved, which drained a
  full network for standing still.
- The tuning turned out better than intended, and the tests were rewritten to say so.
  Directing support costs reserve straight away - 80 banked against 119 over six
  seconds, about the third it diverts - and then pays for itself, 731 against 226 over
  ten times that window, because ground that establishes faster produces more and the
  compounding overtakes what was spent causing it. Attending a front is an investment
  in that front rather than a toll for standing there.
- The first version of that comparison was wrong and passed anyway. The two runs were
  sequential, so the second inherited a more mature and higher-producing field from the
  first and looked better for that reason rather than for the one being measured. It
  read as support costing 79% when the diversion is 35%. Both windows are now taken
  from a field rebuilt the same way.
- Standing off your own ground directs nothing, since support poured into ground the
  network does not hold would be a way to lose Biomass by walking.
- 314 passing across both suites, no failures.


## Phase 6: three permanent developments at one price

- Rapid Rooting establishes held ground faster, Storage Tissue banks more before the
  network overflows, and Conductive Roots carries the player's influence further. They
  are expansion, banking, and active control, and they are deliberately temporary: the
  question they exist to answer is whether those are three strategies a player would
  choose between, before any real physiology is authored.
- One price for all three, rising with how many have been bought and never with which.
  The doc is explicit that the player should be asking what the organism needs rather
  than which line is cheapest, and equal pricing is the only way to stop that question
  from having a wrong answer.
- Each one answers on the battlefield the moment it is bought, because the doc will not
  accept a development that only changes a number. Rapid Rooting runs its own effect
  once at strength, so young Green visibly thickens; Storage Tissue forms an organ at
  every component that holds a reserve; Conductive Roots throws up the new reach at the
  size it now is and leaves it there for a moment.
- Conductive Roots widens the aiming ring by exactly what it widens the release by,
  since an aim that showed the old reach would lie about the effect.
- The drawer is one surface with two tenants. Under the root economy its three buttons
  are the developments; with the flag off they are the three old axes. Reusing the
  panel rather than authoring a second one keeps the comparison honest, because both
  economies are then bought the same way in the same place.
- The captions had to be cut to two or three words. The panel was laid out for
  "More material" and "Push harder", and a one-sentence effect there ran into the
  button beside it. The sentence lives in the tooltip and the status line instead.
- A purchase that cannot be paid in full puts back what it took rather than granting
  half a development.
- Two of the Phase 6 checks were measuring nothing until they were fixed. Buying emits
  the development signal, which the run answers by re-applying its modifiers, so a
  maturation reading taken after the purchase was already the developed one and was
  being compared with itself; and the reserve is capacity-bound, so a full-price
  purchase left too little for the two after it to be bought at all.
- 320 passing across both suites, no failures.


## Phase 7: the root system moves onto the battlefield

- The root network is drawn where it lives: each organism a heart with vessels running
  out of it, pulses riding those vessels, and storage organs that fill as the reserves
  do. The HUD summarises it and never replaces it, and no permanent root panel was
  added, which is what the doc asks for.
- Flow stops where the organism ends. A colony that has been cut off keeps its roots
  and loses its pulses, and that absence is the whole message: the doc is explicit that
  a severed region must not instantly look dead, and stopping is what says it is
  isolated without a line of text saying so. This was the half of the sever
  visualisation that Phase 3 could not build, because there were no pulses yet.
- A release now runs hot through the vessels before it reaches the border, so what was
  spent is seen leaving the organism rather than only arriving at the frontier. That
  was the other thing Phase 4 had to leave until there were roots to carry it.
- Flow favours the ground the player is standing on, drawn from the same demand signal
  the economy reads rather than described separately.
- The drawn layer is rebuilt on the topology cadence, not per frame. Sampling the field
  every frame would cost a full pass to show something that changes twice a second, and
  the vessels are sampled with a stride for the same reason: a vessel is a broad shape,
  and touching every cell to draw a few dozen lines buys nothing visible.
- Root Inspect is held rather than toggled and does not stop the run, so it cannot be
  left up by accident while the battlefield needs the player. It brightens the layer and
  labels each component with its own reserve, drawn at that component: a colony's
  Biomass belongs to a place on the battlefield, not to a sidebar row.
- The permanent Biomass line gained the ceiling it can actually hit, and turns amber at
  it, because a full network is throwing production away and that is a state worth
  seeing without reading. A development being affordable buds the line rather than
  brightening the dot already there: the doc asks for a change of silhouette, so it
  cannot be confused with the other reason something is spendable.
- The Phase 7 HUD check was calling the wrong thing and testing nothing. The Biomass
  line is written from Progression's biomass signal, which the root economy mirrors
  every frame, not from the HUD refresh, so refreshing the HUD left the old text in
  place. It is driven the way the run drives it now.
- 326 passing across both suites, no failures.

## Two things on the battlefield that did not say what they meant

- The dashed ring marked a colony without saying it was one. A marker nobody can read
  is decoration, so it names itself now and carries the number that makes it matter:
  `CUT OFF  166`, Biomass the run is producing and cannot spend. Colonies that start a
  run already isolated never fired a sever event and so were never explained at all,
  which is how one ended up sitting unexplained on the opening screen.
- Its text was 11px over the most saturated colour on screen. The accessibility
  guidance the doc cites treats small text as a defect; it is 17px on a backing plate

- The roots were drawn at an alpha that made them invisible during normal play, which
  made "what are the roots doing" unanswerable before it was even a question about
  flow. Subtle is not the same as not there.
- More importantly they were not doing anything legible. Pulses always ran outward,
  which said nothing. Direction carries the meaning now: inward is the organism
  gathering what its ground produced into the reserve, and outward is the organism
  spending, pushing support to the front the player is standing at. One picture, two
  opposite meanings, and the reversal is the player's own influence becoming visible

- Vessels curve instead of running straight to a hub, so they read as growth rather

## The Clover machine replaces the incremental layer

- Gambling comes out of the secret seventh Path and becomes the run's one interactive
  economy. Biomass is the stake and the payout is a Path event, which is the whole
  point of the swap: what is staked comes off territory and what is won lands on the
  battlefield, so the two are no longer one axis wearing different names. That was the
  fault the root economy could not be tuned out of.
- Reels are the five Paths plus the Clover wild and the Bust, per the original design
  notes. One symbol is a minor effect, two is stronger, three is a Jackpot, and Triple
  Clover fires an event from every Path at once. A Clover stands in for whatever is
  beside it, so two Seeds and a Clover is three Seeds.
- The notched slider is the decision. A bigger wager buys a bigger payout and a worse
  Bust in the same movement - measured at 1.00 severity at the reference stake and 3.00
  at triple - and each notch plays a different reel table, the high one carrying more
  Wilds and more Busts at once. Payout alone would have made the slider a dial with one
  right answer.
- A Bust never deletes territory, as the notes require. It erodes the ground around the
  player and strips what was rooted there, and leaves the run to deal with it.
- The result is rolled when the lever goes down and paid when the reels stop. Paying at
  the pull would have made the reveal a decoration; nothing is emitted mid-spin, and the
  reels land on exactly what was rolled.
- Reels stop left to right, 0.70s, 1.15s, 1.65s. The gap between the second and the
  third is the only tension a slot machine has to offer.
- Each window clips a two-face strip that scrolls through it, so a spin reads as reels
  turning rather than three icons changing. Faces return to the rest positions the scene
  gave them whenever the machine is idle, which is what keeps the layout check honest.
- The lever swaps between its two art frames rather than sliding. Dragging the whole
  sprite down moved the ball, the shaft and the base together, which read as the lever
  falling off the machine. The handle-shortening idea was left out on purpose: animating
  the rect would break the invariant that nothing moves a control the scene placed.
- Metabolism stays as Mass, Push and Hold, and now develops on its own from the run's
  lifetime Biomass rather than being bought. Levels are derived from the authored cost
  curve, so the climb is what buying everything in the best order would have given,
  without the clicking. Paths lean it: Root and Spore toward Mass, Bloom and Pruning
  toward Push, Bramble toward Hold.
- Lifetime earnings are tracked apart from the balance, so a bad spin costs a stake and
  never the organism's growth. The drawer stops being a shop and becomes the readout of
  that development.
- The Photosynthesis button declared no `display_text`, so the tool script rewrote its
  caption from the script default on every load and quietly reverted the scene edit
  three times before it was spotted.
- 334 passing across both suites, no failures.


## Summary

**Bloom.** The splatter finally dents hostile ground. Three reported attempts failed
because I kept fixing what I assumed rather than what I measured: the limiter was not
Mass and not the maturity-break coefficient but the landing footprint, which was the
glob's own in-flight radius. Paint flattens on impact, so landings now spread by
their radius and the root of their speed — **55 cells dented, then 443**. Bloom is
throw-led: the material is split rather than duplicated, droplet count and size are
tied by conservation instead of scaling independently, and base strength was
calibrated back to the pre-splatter Bloom, measured on open ground and on a matured
front. Globs run down walls and rocks instead of vanishing into them, and a glob
landing on a germinating Seed establishes it.

**The interface.** New progress-bar art on the health, territory, level and ecosystem
meters; the ecosystem axes each show what they do to the border — material arriving,
a shove, a knitting-together — where two of the three had no visual at all; releases
pour in over a ramp rather than jumping between two frames.

**The rule that cost the most.** What the scene declares is what the game must show.
I broke it repeatedly today — a tool script recomputing rects that had been placed by
hand, placeholders describing older versions of their own lines, code writing text the
editor disagreed with — and had to be told more than once. There are now two checks
for it: every placeholder must match the shape the game writes, and nothing may move a
control the scene placed by hand. They name the offending node, and running them after
any interface work is not optional.

**What I would do differently.** Three separate times a change was reverted because
the scene was open in the editor while I wrote to it; I re-did the same work instead of
recognising the pattern on the first revert. And a cosmetic detail — a format string
written as a literal newline rather than an escape — took several rounds for no gain.
Both cost far more of the session than the work they were attached to.
